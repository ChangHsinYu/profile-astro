        **Feedback**
        This series originated from my interest in the mechanism of acoustic feedback between microphones and loudspeakers: sound emitted by a loudspeaker is picked up again by a microphone, causing the system’s output to become the input for its next cycle of operation and to change continuously through repetition. One of the most compelling aspects of this phenomenon is that it does not require the deliberate introduction of any additional external input. Even the faintest vibrations in the air, or the background noise of the circuitry itself, may be amplified through repeated feedback and eventually develop into an immense sound. These sounds also vary according to the structure, materials, and acoustic properties of the space, allowing the space itself to participate in their generation. This led me to consider whether a work might begin not from the direct production of content, but by incorporating existing phenomena, environmental changes, and contingent factors produced through audience intervention, thereby shifting artistic creation from the production of content toward the construction of rules and relationships. The work would no longer be a predetermined result, but a system that operates autonomously within a given framework and continues to change over time. Space and environment would likewise cease to function merely as sites in which the work is placed and instead become part of its constitution.

        *Feeding (2020)* consists of several acoustic feedback units. Each unit contains a movable microphone and loudspeaker, as well as a sensor that detects sound intensity. When the sound becomes too quiet, the microphone descends and moves closer to the loudspeaker, intensifying the feedback. When the sound becomes too loud, the microphone rises and moves farther away, weakening it. The intensity of the sound thus feeds back into and regulates its own feedback condition, keeping the system in a continuous process of seeking equilibrium.
        *Cross (2021)* uses four sets of microphones and loudspeakers to form an acoustic feedback loop. After the microphone in the first unit receives the sound emitted by its loudspeaker, it transmits the signal to the loudspeaker in the next unit. The signal passes sequentially through all four units before returning to the initial loudspeaker. During each transmission, the sound undergoes subtle changes caused by the frequency responses of the different loudspeakers, spatial reflections, and audience intervention. These changes repeatedly re-enter the system with each cycle, causing the sound to deform continuously and generate results that cannot be precisely anticipated.
        *Cyberfly (2021)* consists of a small motor, a single-blade propeller, a counterweight, and a microphone suspended above a loudspeaker. The imbalance produced during rotation causes the device to twist continuously, while the microphone attached to its end constantly changes its distance and angle in relation to the loudspeaker. These unstable, interconnected movements continually interfere with the formation of acoustic feedback, causing feedback that might otherwise intensify indefinitely to emerge and recede intermittently, producing low-frequency sounds at irregular intervals.
        *Tree Noise (2022)* attaches a contact microphone to the trunk of a tree, receiving vibrations and sounds transmitted through the interior of the tree and from its surrounding environment. These sounds are played through several mushroom-shaped devices distributed around the tree. They are then picked up again by the microphone on the trunk from varying distances and reintroduced into the system, forming a cycle jointly constituted by the tree, the environment, and the devices.

        **Wrong Sound**
        The works described above belong to a series of experiments that I previously referred to as “Wrong Sound.” In the conventional use of sound systems, many phenomena must be avoided in order to preserve signal quality, operational stability, and equipment safety. Feedback is one of them. When phenomena originally regarded as technical problems become the principal sonic material of a work, can “error” itself become an object of listening? *&#x611b* extends this question by employing another phenomenon that is normally avoided: the turn-on/turn-off transient produced when audio equipment is switched on or off. When equipment is not powered on or shut down in the recommended sequence, or when the power supply is directly connected and disconnected, sudden fluctuations in current may be transmitted through the amplifier to the loudspeaker, producing a brief and forceful popping sound that may, under certain conditions, damage the loudspeaker.

        In *&#x611b (2020)* , continuously generated text from online forums is converted into binary signals through UTF-8 encoding. The values 0 and 1 directly control whether the loudspeaker is powered on or off, and the electrical transients generated through these switches form the sound.

        Here, the loudspeaker no longer performs its conventional function of reproducing a prepared audio signal, but instead produces sound through the act of switching the power supply itself. Does the so-called “error” reside in the sound itself, or does it arise from the technical system’s assumptions about proper operation? When a phenomenon that is normally excluded is removed from established conventions of use and becomes the primary sonic source of a work, are we listening to an error, to the exposure of the physical operation of a technical device, or to another mode of listening constructed by technical rules?

        However, from Luigi Russolo’s incorporation of industrial and urban noise into musical vocabulary, to Steve Reich’s use of swinging microphones to allow acoustic feedback to unfold through pendular motion, and Alvin Lucier’s explorations of the relationships between sound, space, and resonance, noise, feedback, and acoustic environments possess an artistic lineage far longer than my own practice. During the development of these works, Adam Basanta became one of my more direct contemporary references. By moving microphones, changing the distance between microphones and loudspeakers, or employing the acoustic properties of a space, he uses feedback as a mechanism for revealing relationships among devices, space, and listening. Looking back at my own works, I once attempted to establish distinctions through the forms of the devices, more complex operational logic, and connections between the works and personal experience. Yet I still do not consider these differences sufficient to constitute a substantive theoretical separation. For me, the more important question no longer seems to be how to prove that these works differ from existing practices, but rather what directions they subsequently opened within my own practice.

        **Uncertainty**
        This question gradually led me away from works centred primarily on loudspeaker systems and allowed me to understand these early experiments as an entry point into a broader investigation. What mattered was no longer feedback or electrical transients themselves, but how uncertain factors from different sources enter a predetermined set of rules and alter the development of a system. Uncertainty and the loosening of the author’s control over results have long been important concerns in experimental music, particularly in John Cage’s practice of indeterminacy. I therefore do not regard this as an original idea of my own. What I seek to ask is how this mode of thought—one that does not completely determine its outcome—actually takes place when materially implemented within different technological and physical systems. Do different sources of uncertainty also correspond to different forms and degrees of authorial control?

        In the works described above, uncertain factors already appear in different forms. *Feeding* interrupts mechanical repetition through irregular pauses, while interactions among multiple units produce unstable conditions that are difficult to anticipate. *Cross* is affected by the frequency responses of loudspeakers with different specifications, spatial reflections, and disturbances caused by audience intervention. *Cyberfly* generates unstable twisting movements through an imbalanced mechanical structure. *Tree Noise* incorporates a more complex external environment into the cycle. In *&#x611b* , continuously changing online text makes it impossible to determine the binary sequence entering the system in advance.

        In the later works *Soul (2022)* and *Soul-vinyl (2023)* , the audience activates an electrostatic-generating device upon entering the exhibition space. Electromagnetic sensing devices receive signals from the surrounding electromagnetic environment and transmit them to a performance system, triggering different instruments. I predetermine the chords and rhythmic rules available to the system, while the actual development of the music is shaped by continuously fluctuating electromagnetic signals.
        In *Microbial Ensemble (2026)* , electrical signals measured from microbial systems in soil drive a physical jazz drum kit. The structure of the system and the instruments are predetermined by me, while the timing and conditions under which sounds occur are influenced by microbial activity that cannot be precisely predicted.

        Seen through these works, not determining an outcome does not imply the withdrawal of the author. It is better understood as a redistribution of the location of control. I still determine the basic structure of the system, the sensing methods, the conditions under which signals enter the system, and the sonic parameters on which they are ultimately able to act. Yet once these rules have been established, when a signal appears, how it fluctuates, and what result the system produces at a given moment are no longer entirely determined by me. Different sources of uncertainty also generate different relations of control. Artificially configured random mechanisms, such as preset random functions in software, can still be clearly bounded in advance. By contrast, changes produced by mechanical structures, spaces, environments, or biological activity only emerge through the actual contact of the work with material and environmental conditions during operation. This establishes a spectrum between controllability and uncontrollability within my work. It has also helped me recognise a misconception in my earlier thinking: perhaps a work can never truly escape control, but can only be co-constructed to different degrees.

        Within the acoustic feedback series, sound is not merely the system’s final result. It also becomes a means of sensing the site and its changes, exposing how space, devices, and rules are acting upon one another. In later works, when microbial activity, electromagnetic signals, online information, audience behaviour, and other factors encounter the mechanisms, sensors, transformations, and methods of sound production established by the work, sound becomes an event generated through their encounter. Listening is no longer simply the reception of the sound output by the work, but a means of perceiving the interactions of different phenomena within the system. Because these systems operate in real time, each act of listening is a partial capture of an ongoing autonomous process.

        **Autonomous System**
        “Autonomy” is also a concept that requires further clarification here. Within this series, autonomy currently refers primarily to operational autonomy, along with a limited degree of self-regulation. The system’s basic rules are still established by me, but once activated, its operation and sonic results no longer depend on direct human manipulation. In most of the existing works, uncertain factors also function only as inputs into the system, influencing its subsequent sonic and operational states. This leaves a question that has not yet been fully developed within the current works: if the results produced by a system could in turn alter its own mode of operation, could uncertainty and feedback form a more complex relationship of self-regulation, or even approach the possibility of the system modifying its own rules through self-modification?

        When the rules of a work are repeatedly altered by the results it generates during its continued operation, what relationship does the system retain with the one originally established by the artist after long periods of iteration, like the Ship of Theseus? At that point, autonomy may no longer refer only to whether a system can continue operating without human intervention, but may begin to concern the extent to which the control initially established by the artist can determine the work’s subsequent development. When this relation no longer occurs solely within a single system, but begins to take place among multiple systems governed by different operational rules, the boundary of the system itself also becomes a question.

        **System Boundaries**
        Several works in this series consist of multiple units governed by the same operational logic. These units are not always closed systems; they may affect one another through shared space, signals, or environments. In Feeding, the rule controlling the distance between the microphone and loudspeaker exists within each individual unit. However, when eight devices operate simultaneously within the same space, the sound emitted by each unit may also be received by others, allowing feedback systems that were originally operating independently to interfere with one another through a shared acoustic environment. In Cross, this relationship is directly built into the system’s structure. Each of the four units can be understood as an individual sound-reception and amplification device. Yet when the output of one unit becomes the input of the next, the units continuously affect one another through the loop. In Soul and Soul-vinyl, the three principal units each control a different instrument. They do not directly exchange signals, but become related because they are all affected by the electromagnetic environment of the exhibition space. In Microbial Ensemble, because the sensing system remains open to changes in the surrounding environment, vibrations or other disturbances produced when one device is triggered may also affect how other devices detect and interpret signals. The units in these works exist within a shared environment with varying degrees of openness. Some of their relationships are established by the system’s rules, while others are indirectly formed through space and environment.

        Although the current works expand the number of units, these units essentially still operate within the same set of rules. Yet if the result produced by one system were able to become the input of another, or if systems could alter one another’s operation through shared environments, signals, or materials, could relationships originally contained within a single system extend outward and generate more complex interactions among different systems? Under such conditions, each system would retain its own operational rules while simultaneously becoming part of the environment inhabited by other systems. The overall condition would no longer be determined by any single system, but would emerge through processes of partial interaction among multiple systems. When system and environment can no longer be clearly separated, the boundary of a system may cease to refer solely to a physical extent and begin to involve how we identify, describe, and distinguish different agents and the relationships between them.

        **Magic**
        Magic is usually composed of materials, spells, emotions, and effects. If these elements are mapped onto my works, the materials are the media and substances I use; the spells are the rules and systems that cause them to interact; the emotions are the motivations that lead me to establish these relationships; and sound is the effect produced through their encounter. The preceding discussion has attempted to clarify how I configure control through systems, introduce uncertainty, and bring different materials and phenomena into relation. For me, however, “magic” may be the original motivation behind artistic creation: placing an imagined possibility into the material and technological relations of reality, establishing a set of rules, waiting for it to produce an effect that even I cannot fully predict, and then observing how these effects return to respond to the initial emotion and imagination.

        **Feedback**
        這系列的起源來自我對麥克風與喇叭之間聲音回授（feedback）機制的興趣：喇叭輸出的聲音再次被麥克風接收，系統的輸出成為下一次運作的輸入，並在循環中不斷改變。這個現象迷人的地方之一，在於它不需要刻意加入其他外部輸入，任何空氣中微弱的振動，甚至電路本身的底噪，都可能在反覆回授的過程中被放大，最終衍生出巨大的聲響。這些聲響又會隨著空間結構、材質與聲學特性產生差異，使空間本身也參與聲音的生成。這讓我開始思考，作品是否可以不以直接生產內容為出發點，而是將原本已經存在的現象、環境的變化以及觀眾介入所產生的偶發因素納入作品之中，將創作從創造內容轉向建構規則與關係。作品不再是一個預先決定的結果，而是一套在既定框架中自主運作並持續變化的系統。空間與環境也不再只是放置作品的場域，而成為了作品構成的一部分。
        
        *Feeding (2020)* 由數個聲音回授單元組成，每個單元包含一組可移動的麥克風與喇叭，以及偵測聲音強度的感測器。當聲音過小時，麥克風會下降並靠近喇叭使回授增強；當聲音過大時，麥克風則上升並遠離喇叭使回授減弱。聲音的強度反過來調節自身的回授狀態，使系統持續處於尋找平衡的狀態。
        *Cross (2021)* 由四組麥克風與喇叭構成一個聲音回授迴路。第一組麥克風接收到喇叭播放的聲音後，將訊號傳送至下一組喇叭，依序經過四個單元，最後再回到最初的喇叭。聲音在每一次傳遞中受到不同喇叭的頻率響應、空間反射與觀眾介入的影響而產生細微變化，這些變化隨著循環不斷重新進入系統，使聲音持續變形並產生無法精確預期的結果。
        *Cyberfly (2021)* 由小型馬達、單片螺旋槳、配重與麥克風構成，並懸吊於喇叭上方。旋轉時產生的不平衡使裝置持續扭動，尾端連接的麥克風也不斷改變與喇叭之間的距離和角度。這些不穩定連動產生的變化持續干擾聲音回授的產生，使原本可能不斷增強的回授時而形成、時而消退，產生不固定出現的低頻聲響。
        *Tree Noise (2022)* 將接觸式麥克風固定於樹幹，接收樹木內部與周遭環境傳導至樹幹上的震動與聲響，透過分布於樹木周圍的數個蕈狀裝置播放。這些聲音又再次以不同距離被樹幹上的麥克風接收，重新進入系統，使樹木、環境與裝置共同形成循環。

        **Wrong Sound**
        上述作品皆屬於我過往稱為「錯誤的聲音」的一系列實驗作品。在音響系統的正常使用中，為了維持訊號品質、設備穩定與器材安全，存在許多需要被避免的現象，回授便是其中之一。當這些原本被視為技術問題的現象成為作品主要的聲響，「錯誤」本身是否也能成為聆聽的對象？ *&#x611b* 延續了這個問題，使用了另一種通常需要被避免的現象：音響設備開關機時可能產生的瞬間訊號（turn-on/turn-off transient）。當設備未依照建議的順序開啟或關閉，或電源被直接接通與切斷時，瞬間的電流變化可能經由擴大器傳送至喇叭，形成短促而強烈的爆裂聲（pop），在特定情況下也可能對喇叭造成損害。

        在 *&#x611b (2020)* 中我將網路論壇中持續產生的文字透過 UTF-8 編碼轉換為二進位訊號，並以 0 與 1 直接控制喇叭的通電與斷電，電源切換所產生的突波形成聲響。

        喇叭在這裡不再依照原本的用途播放一段被製作好的聲音訊號，而是透過電源切換本身發聲。所謂「錯誤」究竟存在於聲音本身，還是來自技術系統對正常運作方式的預設？當原本需要被排除的現象脫離既定的使用規範，成為作品主要的聲音來源時，我們所聆聽的究竟是一個錯誤、一套技術裝置自身物理運作的顯露，還是另一種由技術規則所建構的聆聽方式？

        然而，從 Luigi Russolo 將工業與城市噪音納入音樂語彙，到 Steve Reich 透過麥克風的鐘擺運動，使聲音回授隨著麥克風擺動自行展開，以及 Alvin Lucier 對聲音、空間與共鳴關係的探索，噪音、回授與聲學環境都有著比我自身實踐更為悠久的藝術史脈絡。在我實際發展這些作品的過程中，Adam Basanta 則是更為直接的當代參照之一。他透過移動麥克風、改變麥克風與喇叭之間的距離，或利用空間自身的聲學特性，使聲音回授成為揭露裝置、空間與聆聽關係的機制。回頭檢視自己的作品，我過去曾試圖透過裝置造型、更複雜的運作邏輯，以及作品與個人經驗之間的連結來建立差異，但我依舊認為這些差異並不足以構成實質的理論區隔。對我而言，更重要的問題似乎不再是如何證明這些作品與既有實踐有何不同，而是它們後來將我的創作帶往了什麼方向。

        **Uncertainty**       
        這個問題使我後來逐漸離開以喇叭系統為主要機制的作品，也使我開始將前期實驗理解為更廣泛研究的入口：重要的不再只是回授或突波本身，而是不同來源的不確定因素如何進入一套既定規則，並改變系統的發展。不確定性以及作者對結果控制的鬆動在 John Cage 對 indeterminacy 的實踐中已是實驗音樂的重要命題，因此我並不把這視為我原創的想法，我想追問的是，當這種不完全決定結果的思考被具體實現在不同的技術與物質系統中時，它究竟如何發生？不同來源的不確定性是否也對應著不同形式與程度的作者控制？在上述作品中，不確定因素已經以不同形式存在。 *Feeding* 透過不固定的暫停時間打破機械性的重複、多個單元的互相影響導致難以預期的不穩定狀態； *Cross* 受到不同規格喇叭的頻率響應、空間反射與觀眾介入的擾動； *Cyberfly* 透過不平衡的機械結構產生不穩定的扭動； *Tree Noise* 將更複雜的外在環境納入循環； *&#x611b* 即時變動的網路文字使進入系統的二進位序列無法被預先決定。
        
        後續作品 *Soul (2022)* , *Soul-vinyl (2023)* 中，觀眾進入展場後觸發起電裝置產生靜電，電磁感測裝置接收空間中的電磁訊號，並將訊號傳送至演奏系統，觸發不同的樂器。我預先設定系統可以使用的和弦與節奏規則，而音樂實際的發展則受到持續波動的電磁訊號影響。
        *Microbial Ensemble (2026)* 中，我則利用泥土中微生物系統中測量到的電訊號驅動一組實體爵士鼓。系統的結構與樂器由我預先設定，而聲音實際發生的時間與狀態則受到微生物無法被精確預測的活動影響。

        從這些作品來看，不決定結果並不意味著作者的退出，更像是控制位置的重新分配。我仍然決定系統的基本結構、感測方式、訊號進入系統的條件，以及它最終能夠作用於哪些聲音參數，但在這些規則建立之後，訊號何時出現、如何波動，以及系統在特定時刻形成什麼結果，則不再完全由我決定。不同來源的不確定性也產生不同的控制關係，人為設定的隨機機制，例如程式中預設的隨機函數，其範圍仍可以事先被明確界定，而機械結構、空間、環境或生物活動所產生的變化，則必須透過作品實際運作時與物質及環境的接觸才會發生。這在我的作品中建立了一條介於可控與不可控的光譜，而這條光譜也幫助我發現了我過往想法的一個誤區，也許作品是不可能真正擺脫控制的，最多是不同程度的共構。
        
        在聲音回授系列創作中，聲音不只是系統最終產生的結果，更成為了感受場域及其變化的線索，它同時暴露了空間、裝置與規則正在如何彼此作用。在後續作品中，當微生物活動、電磁訊號、網路資訊、觀眾的行為等不同因素遇上作品所建立的機制、感測、轉換及發聲方式，聲音也成為了這些因素彼此相遇時所發生的事件。聆聽不再只是接收作品所輸出的聲音，而是透過聲音察覺不同現象在系統中的相互作用。由於這些系統的即時運作，每一次聆聽都是對其自主運作過程的一次局部截取。

        **Autonomous system**        
        「自主」在這邊也是需要進一步釐清的概念。在這個系列中的「自主」目前主要指向運作上的自主（operational autonomy），以及小部分的自我調節（self-regulation），系統的基本規則仍由我建立，只是啟動之後其運作與聲音結果不仰賴人為操作。而現有作品中的不確定因素多半也只作為系統的輸入，影響其後續的聲音與運作狀態；這也留下了一個尚未在目前作品中充分展開的問題：如果系統產生的結果能進一步反過來改變自身的運作方式，不確定性與回授之間是否可能形成更複雜的自我調節關係，甚至進一步觸及系統修改自身規則（self-modification）的可能？當作品的規則在持續運作中被自身產生的結果反覆修改，經過長時間的迭代之後，如同忒修斯之船，它與藝術家最初建立的系統之間還保有什麼關係？此時「自主」或許就不再只是系統能否在沒有人為操作的情況下持續運作，而開始涉及作者最初建立的控制能在多大程度上決定作品後續的發展。而當這種關係不只發生於單一系統內部，開始存在於多個具有不同運作規則的系統之間時，系統本身的邊界也隨之成為問題。

        **System Boundaries**
        在這個系列中，有幾件作品由多個具有同樣運作邏輯的單元所構成，這些單元並不總是封閉，它們可能透過共享的空間、訊號或環境相互影響。在 *Feeding* 中，麥克風與喇叭距離的控制規則存在於每一個單元內部，然而當八座裝置同時存在於同一空間時，每個單元所發出的聲音也可能被其他單元接收，使原本各自運作的回授系統透過共享的聲學環境彼此干擾；在 *Cross* 中，這種關係則被直接建立於系統的結構之中。四個單元各自都可以被理解為一組收音與擴音裝置，但當一個單元的輸出成為下一個單元的輸入，各單元便會在循環中持續影響彼此；在 *Soul* 與 *Soul-vinyl* 中，三個主要單元各自控制不同的樂器，彼此之間沒有直接交換訊號，卻因為共同受到展場中的電磁環境影響而產生關聯； *Microbial Ensemble* 中也可以觀察到由於感測系統對周遭變化保持開放，一座裝置被觸發後所產生的震動或其他環境擾動，也可能影響其他裝置對訊號的判定。這些作品中的單元以不同程度的開放性存在於共享的環境之中；它們之間的關係有些由系統規則建立，有些透過空間與環境間接形成。

        現階段的作品雖然擴展了單元的數量，但這些單元本質上仍在同一套規則中運作。然而，如果一個系統產生的結果能成為另一個系統的輸入，或透過共享的環境、訊號與物質改變其他系統的運作，原本存在於單一系統內部的關係是否可能向外延伸，在不同系統之間形成更複雜的交互作用？這樣的狀態下，每個系統仍然保有自身的運作規則，卻同時成為其他系統所處環境的一部分；整體狀態不再由任何單一系統決定，而是在多個系統彼此局部作用的過程中形成。當系統與環境不再能被清楚分開時，系統的邊界或許也不再只是物理上的範圍，而開始涉及我們如何辨認、描述與劃分不同主體與他們之間的關係。

        **Magic**
        魔法通常由材料、咒語、情感與效果所構成，如果將它們對應到我的作品，材料是我所使用的媒介與物質，咒語是使它們彼此作用的規則與系統，情感是促使我建立這些關係的動機，聲音則是它們相遇之後產生的效果。前面的討論試圖釐清我是如何透過系統配置控制、引入不確定性，並使不同的物質與現象產生關係。而「魔法」對我而言或許正是創作的原始動機：將一個想像放進現實物質與技術的關係之中，建立一套規則，等待它產生某種連我自己也無法完全預知的效果，再觀察這些效果如何回過頭來回應最初的情感與想像。
