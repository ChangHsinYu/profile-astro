        **Feedback**
        This series began with my interest in acoustic feedback between microphones and loudspeakers: sound emitted by a loudspeaker is picked up again by a microphone, causing the system’s output to become the input for its next cycle and to continuously transform through repetition. One of the things I find compelling about this phenomenon is that it does not necessarily require any deliberate external input. Even the slightest vibration in the air, or the background noise of the circuitry itself, can be amplified through repeated feedback and eventually develop into an overwhelming sound. These sounds also vary according to the structure, materials, and acoustic properties of the space, allowing the space itself to participate in their generation.

        This led me to ask whether a work might begin not from the direct production of content, but from incorporating already existing phenomena, environmental changes, and contingencies introduced by the audience. In this sense, my practice shifted from creating content toward constructing rules and relationships. The work is no longer a predetermined result, but a system that operates and continuously changes within an established framework. Space and environment are no longer merely the site in which the work is placed, but become part of the work itself.

        *Feeding (2020)* consists of several acoustic feedback units, each containing a movable microphone and loudspeaker together with a sensor that measures sound intensity. When the sound level becomes too low, the microphone moves downward toward the loudspeaker, increasing the feedback. When the sound becomes too loud, the microphone moves upward and away from it, reducing the feedback. The intensity of the sound therefore regulates the conditions of its own feedback, keeping the system in a continuous process of seeking equilibrium.
        *Cross (2021)* forms an acoustic feedback loop using four sets of microphones and loudspeakers. After the first microphone receives sound from a loudspeaker, the signal is transmitted to the next loudspeaker, passes sequentially through all four units, and eventually returns to the first. During each transmission, the sound undergoes subtle changes caused by the frequency response of the different loudspeakers, spatial reflections, and audience intervention. These variations repeatedly re-enter the system through the loop, causing the sound to continuously transform and producing results that cannot be precisely predicted.
        *Cyberfly (2021)* consists of a small motor, a single-bladed propeller, a counterweight, and a microphone suspended above a loudspeaker. The imbalance generated by the rotating structure causes the device to twist continuously, while the microphone attached to its end constantly changes its distance and angle in relation to the loudspeaker. These unstable mechanical interactions continually interfere with the formation of acoustic feedback, causing feedback that might otherwise intensify continuously to emerge and recede unpredictably, producing intermittent low-frequency sounds.
        *Tree Noise (2022)* places a contact microphone on the trunk of a tree to capture vibrations and sounds transmitted through the tree itself and its surrounding environment. These signals are reproduced through several mushroom-shaped devices distributed around the tree. The sounds are then picked up again by the microphone on the trunk from varying distances and re-enter the system, forming a continuous acoustic feedback loop between the tree, the environment, and the devices.

        **Wrong Sound**
        The works described above belong to a series of experiments that I previously referred to as Wrong Sound. In the normal operation of an audio system, many phenomena are deliberately avoided in order to maintain signal quality, equipment stability, and hardware safety. Acoustic feedback is one of them. When phenomena normally regarded as technical problems become the primary source of sound in a work, can the “error” itself become an object of listening? &#x611b extends this question through another phenomenon that is generally meant to be avoided: the transient signal that can occur when audio equipment is powered on or off. When devices are switched on or off without following the recommended sequence, or when power is abruptly connected and disconnected, sudden changes in current can travel through an amplifier to the loudspeaker, producing a brief and forceful popping sound. Under certain conditions, this can also damage the loudspeaker.

        In *&#x611b (2020)* , I converted continuously generated text from an online forum into binary data through UTF-8 encoding and used the values 0 and 1 to directly control the loudspeaker’s power supply. The electrical transients produced by this switching process became the sound itself.

        Here, the loudspeaker no longer performs its conventional role of reproducing a prepared audio signal. Instead, it produces sound through the act of switching power itself. Does the “error” lie in the sound, or in the technical system’s assumptions about what constitutes normal operation? When a phenomenon that is normally meant to be eliminated is removed from its prescribed mode of use and becomes the primary source of sound in a work, what exactly are we listening to: an error, the physical operation of a technical device making itself perceptible, or another mode of listening constructed through technical norms?

        Yet noise, feedback, and acoustic environments have a much longer artistic history than my own practice. From Luigi Russolo’s incorporation of industrial and urban noise into musical vocabulary, to Steve Reich’s use of the pendular motion of microphones to allow acoustic feedback to unfold through a physical process, and Alvin Lucier’s explorations of sound, space, and resonance, these concerns have long existed within experimental music and sound art. During the development of my own works, Adam Basanta became one of the more direct contemporary references. Through moving microphones, altering the distance between microphones and loudspeakers, and using the acoustic properties of space itself, he employs feedback as a mechanism for revealing relationships among devices, space, and listening. Looking back at my own work, I had previously tried to establish differences through sculptural form, more complex operational logic, and connections between the works and personal experience. I nevertheless came to feel that these differences were insufficient to constitute a substantial theoretical distinction. For me, the more important question was no longer how to prove that these works differed from existing practices, but where they subsequently led my practice.

        **Uncertainty**
        This question gradually led me away from works in which loudspeaker systems were the primary mechanism, and allowed me to understand these earlier experiments as an entry point into a broader inquiry. What became important was no longer feedback or electrical transients themselves, but how uncertainties arising from different sources enter an established set of rules and alter the development of a system. Indeterminacy and the loosening of the author’s control over outcomes had already become important concerns in experimental music through John Cage’s practice, so I do not consider this idea itself original to my work. What I want to ask instead is: when the idea of not fully determining an outcome is materially implemented within different technical and physical systems, how does it actually occur? Do different sources of uncertainty correspond to different forms and degrees of authorial control?

        In the works described above, uncertainty is already present in different forms. Feeding interrupts mechanical repetition through irregular pauses, while interactions among multiple units create unstable states that are difficult to predict. Cross is affected by the differing frequency responses of loudspeakers, spatial reflections, and audience intervention. Cyberfly generates instability through an unbalanced mechanical structure. Tree Noise incorporates a more complex external environment into its feedback loop. In &#x611b, continuously changing text from an online forum means that the binary sequences entering the system cannot be predetermined.

        In *Soul (2022)* and *Soul-vinyl (2023)* , the presence of visitors triggers an electrostatic device, generating static electricity. Electromagnetic sensors receive signals in the surrounding space and transmit them to a performance system, which in turn triggers different instruments. I predefine the harmonic and rhythmic rules available to the system, while the actual development of the music is influenced by the continuously fluctuating electromagnetic signals.
        In *Microbial Ensemble (2026)* , I use electrical signals measured from microbial systems in soil to drive a physical jazz drum kit. The structure of the system and the instruments are predetermined by me, while the timing and state of the resulting sounds are influenced by microbial activity that cannot be precisely predicted.

        Looking across these works, not determining the outcome does not mean that the author disappears. It is better understood as a redistribution of control. I still determine the fundamental structure of the system, the methods of sensing, the conditions under which signals enter the system, and the sound parameters upon which they are allowed to act. Once these rules have been established, however, the timing of signals, the ways in which they fluctuate, and the particular state the system takes at any given moment are no longer entirely determined by me. Different sources of uncertainty also produce different relationships to control. Artificially programmed randomness, such as a random function in software, can still have its possible range explicitly defined in advance. By contrast, variations generated through mechanical structures, space, environment, or biological activity only emerge through the work’s actual contact with material and environmental conditions during operation. This has created, across my works, a spectrum between the controllable and the uncontrollable. It has also made me recognize a limitation in my earlier thinking: a work can never entirely escape control, but can only be constituted through different degrees and distributions of it.

        In the feedback works, sound is not merely the final result of the system. It becomes a way of sensing a field and its changes, while simultaneously revealing how space, devices, and rules are acting upon one another. In later works, when microbial activity, electromagnetic signals, online information, audience behavior, and other factors encounter the mechanisms, sensing processes, transformations, and sound-producing methods established by the work, sound increasingly becomes an event produced through their encounter. Listening is therefore no longer simply the reception of sound output, but a way of perceiving how different phenomena interact within a system. Because these systems operate in real time, each act of listening is only a partial sampling of an ongoing process.

        **Autonomous System**
        “Autonomy” is another concept that requires further clarification here. Within this series, an “autonomous system” currently refers primarily to operational autonomy and, in some works, to forms of self-regulation. The basic rules of the system are still established by me, but once activated, its operation and sonic outcomes no longer depend on continuous human intervention. In the existing works, however, uncertain factors still function primarily as inputs that influence the system’s subsequent sonic and operational states. This leaves a question that has not yet been fully developed within the work: if the results generated by a system were able to feed back and alter the way the system itself operates, could uncertainty and feedback form a more complex relationship of self-regulation, or even begin to approach the possibility of self-modification?

        If the rules of a work were repeatedly modified by the results it had previously produced, what relationship would remain, after extended iterations, between the evolving system and the one originally established by the artist? Like the Ship of Theseus, the question would no longer concern only whether the system can continue to function without human operation, but also the extent to which the author’s initial control continues to determine its subsequent development. And when such relationships no longer occur only within a single system, but begin to emerge among multiple systems with different operational rules, the boundary of the system itself also becomes a question.

        **System Boundaries**
        Several works in this series consist of multiple units with their own operational logics, and these units are not always closed. They may affect one another through shared space, signals, or environmental conditions. In *Feeding*, the rule controlling the distance between microphone and loudspeaker exists within each individual unit. Yet when eight units occupy the same space, the sound produced by one unit may also be picked up by another, allowing previously separate feedback systems to interfere with one another through a shared acoustic environment. In *Cross*, this relationship is directly built into the structure of the work. Each of the four units can be understood as an individual system for receiving and amplifying sound, yet once the output of one becomes the input of the next, each continuously influences the others through the loop. In *Soul* and *Soul-vinyl* , three main units control different instruments. They do not exchange signals directly, yet they become related because they are all affected by the electromagnetic environment of the exhibition space. A similar but distinct relationship can be observed in *Microbial Ensemble* . Because the sensing system remains open to changes in its surroundings, the vibration or other environmental disturbance produced when one unit is triggered may affect the way another unit interprets its signals. The units in these works are therefore not fully independent entities. They exist with different degrees of openness within a shared environment. Some of their relationships are established directly by the rules of the system, while others emerge indirectly through space and environmental conditions.

        These practices have also led me to reconsider where the boundary of a system lies. Although the current works have increased the number of units involved, these units still fundamentally operate within a shared set of rules. But what happens if the result of one system becomes the input of another system governed by its own rules, or if one system alters another through shared environments, signals, or materials? Could relationships that initially exist within a single system extend outward and form more complex interactions between different systems? In such a condition, each system would retain its own operational rules while simultaneously becoming part of the environment in which other systems operate. The overall state would no longer be determined by any single system, but would emerge through their partial and local interactions. When system and environment can no longer be clearly separated, the boundary of a system may no longer be understood simply as a physical limit, but as something produced through the way we identify, describe, and distinguish these interacting relations.

        **Magic**
        Magic is often imagined as being composed of materials, spells, emotions, and effects. If I map these elements onto my own practice, the materials are the media and matter I work with; the spells are the rules and systems through which they act upon one another; emotion is the motivation that leads me to construct these relationships; and sound is the effect that emerges from their encounter. The preceding discussion has attempted to clarify how I configure control through systems, introduce uncertainty, and bring different materials and phenomena into relation. For me, however, “magic” may point back to the more fundamental motivation behind the work: placing an imagination within the material and technical conditions of reality, establishing a set of rules, waiting for it to produce an effect that even I cannot fully predict, and then observing how that effect returns to respond to the emotion and imagination from which the work began.

        **Feedback**
        這系列的起源來自我對麥克風與喇叭之間聲音回授（feedback）機制的興趣：喇叭輸出的聲音再次被麥克風接收，系統的輸出成為下一次運作的輸入，並在循環中不斷改變。這個現象迷人的地方之一，在於它不需要刻意加入其他外部輸入，任何空氣中微弱的振動，甚至電路本身的底噪，都可能在反覆回授的過程中被放大，最終衍生出巨大的聲響。這些聲響又會隨著空間結構、材質與聲學特性產生差異，使空間本身也參與聲音的生成。這讓我開始思考，作品是否可以不以直接生產內容為出發點，而是將原本已經存在的現象、環境的變化以及觀眾介入所產生的偶發因素納入作品之中，將創作從創造內容轉向建構規則與關係。作品不再是一個預先決定的結果，而是一套在既定框架中自主運作並持續變化的系統。空間與環境也不再只是放置作品的場域，而成為了作品構成的一部分。
        
        *Feeding (2020)* 由數個聲音回授單元組成，每個單元包含一組可移動的麥克風與喇叭，以及偵測聲音強度的感測器。當聲音過小時，麥克風會下降並靠近喇叭使回授增強；當聲音過大時，麥克風則上升並遠離喇叭使回授減弱。聲音的強度反過來調節自身的回授狀態，使系統持續處於尋找平衡的狀態。
        *Cross (2021)* 由四組麥克風與喇叭構成一個聲音回授迴路。第一組麥克風接收到喇叭播放的聲音後，將訊號傳送至下一組喇叭，依序經過四個單元，最後再回到最初的喇叭。聲音在每一次傳遞中受到不同喇叭的頻率響應、空間反射與觀眾介入的影響而產生細微變化，這些變化隨著循環不斷重新進入系統，使聲音持續變形並產生無法精確預期的結果。
        *Cyberfly (2021)* 由小型馬達、單片螺旋槳、配重與麥克風構成，並懸吊於喇叭上方。旋轉時產生的不平衡使裝置持續扭動，尾端連接的麥克風也不斷改變與喇叭之間的距離和角度。這些不穩定連動產生的變化持續干擾聲音回授的產生，使原本可能不斷增強的回授時而形成、時而消退，產生不固定出現的低頻聲響。
        *Tree Noise (2022)* 將接觸式麥克風固定於樹幹，接收樹木內部與周遭環境傳導至樹幹上的震動與聲響，透過分布於樹木周圍的數個蕈狀裝置播放。這些聲音又再次以不同距離被樹幹上的麥克風接收，重新進入系統，使樹木、環境與裝置共同形成循環。

        **Wrong Sound**
        上述作品皆屬於我過往稱為「錯誤的聲音」的一系列實驗作品。在音響系統的正常使用中，為了維持訊號品質、設備穩定與器材安全，存在許多需要被避免的現象，回授便是其中之一。當這些原本被視為技術問題的現象成為作品主要的聲響，「錯誤」本身是否也能成為聆聽的對象？ *&#x611b* 延續了這個問題，使用了另一種通常需要被避免的現象：音響設備開關機時可能產生的瞬間訊號（turn-on/turn-off transient）。當設備未依照建議的順序開啟或關閉，或電源被直接接通與切斷時，瞬間的電流變化可能經由擴大器傳送至喇叭，形成短促而強烈的爆裂聲（pop），在特定情況下也可能對喇叭造成損害。

        在 *&#x611b (2020)* 中我將網路論壇中持續產生的文字透過 UTF-8 編碼轉換為二進位訊號，並以 0 與 1 直接控制喇叭的通電與斷電，電源切換所產生的突波形成聲響。

        喇叭在這裡不再依照原本的用途播放一段被製作好的聲音訊號，而是透過電源切換本身發聲。所謂「錯誤」究竟存在於聲音本身，還是來自技術系統對正常運作方式的預設？當原本需要被排除的現象脫離既定的使用規範，成為作品主要的聲音來源時，我們所聆聽的究竟是一個錯誤、一套技術裝置自身物理運作的顯露，還是另一種由技術規則所建構的聆聽方式？

        然而，從 Luigi Russolo 將工業與城市噪音納入音樂語彙，到 Steve Reich 透過麥克風的鐘擺運動，使聲音回授隨著麥克風擺動自行展開，以及 Alvin Lucier 對聲音、空間與共鳴關係的探索，噪音、回授與聲學環境都有著比我自身實踐更為悠久的藝術史脈絡。在我實際發展這些作品的過程中，Adam Basanta 則是更為直接的當代參照之一。他透過移動麥克風、改變麥克風與喇叭之間的距離，或利用空間自身的聲學特性，使聲音回授成為揭露裝置、空間與聆聽關係的機制。回頭檢視自己的作品，我過去曾試圖透過裝置造型、更複雜的運作邏輯，以及作品與個人經驗之間的連結來建立差異，但我依舊認為這些差異並不足以構成實質的理論區隔。對我而言，更重要的問題似乎不再是如何證明這些作品與既有實踐有何不同，而是它們後來將我的創作帶往了什麼方向。

        **Uncertainty**       
        這個問題使我後來逐漸離開以喇叭系統為主要機制的作品，也使我開始將前期實驗理解為更廣泛研究的入口：重要的不再只是回授或突波本身，而是不同來源的不確定因素如何進入一套既定規則，並改變系統的發展。不確定性以及作者對結果控制的鬆動在 John Cage 對 indeterminacy 的實踐中已是實驗音樂的重要命題，因此我並不把這視為我原創的想法，我想追問的是，當這種不完全決定結果的思考被具體實現在不同的技術與物質系統中時，它究竟如何發生？不同來源的不確定性是否也對應著不同形式與程度的作者控制？在上述作品中，不確定因素已經以不同形式存在。 *Feeding* 透過不固定的暫停時間打破機械性的重複、多個單元的互相影響導致難以預期的不穩定狀態； *Cross* 受到不同規格喇叭的頻率響應、空間反射與觀眾介入的擾動； *Cyberfly* 透過不平衡的機械結構產生不穩定的扭動； *Tree Noise* 將更複雜的外在環境納入循環； *&#x611b* 即時變動的網路文字使進入系統的二進位序列無法被預先決定。
        
        後續作品 *Soul (2022)* , *Soul-vinyl (2023)* 中，觀眾進入展場後觸發起電裝置產生靜電，電磁感測裝置接收空間中的電磁訊號，並將訊號傳送至演奏系統，觸發不同的樂器。我預先設定系統可以使用的和弦與節奏規則，而音樂實際的發展則受到持續波動的電磁訊號影響。
        *Microbial Ensemble (2026)* 中，我則利用泥土中微生物系統中測量到的電訊號驅動一組實體爵士鼓。系統的結構與樂器由我預先設定，而聲音實際發生的時間與狀態則受到微生物無法被精確預測的活動影響。

        從這些作品來看，不決定結果並不意味著作者的退出，更像是控制位置的重新分配。我仍然決定系統的基本結構、感測方式、訊號進入系統的條件，以及它最終能夠作用於哪些聲音參數，但在這些規則建立之後，訊號何時出現、如何波動，以及系統在特定時刻形成什麼結果，則不再完全由我決定。不同來源的不確定性也產生不同的控制關係，人為設定的隨機機制，例如程式中預設的隨機函數，其範圍仍可以事先被明確界定，而機械結構、空間、環境或生物活動所產生的變化，則必須透過作品實際運作時與物質及環境的接觸才會發生。這在我的作品中建立了一條介於可控與不可控的光譜，而這條光譜也幫助我發現了我過往想法的一個誤區，也許作品是不可能真正擺脫控制的，最多是不同程度的共構。
        
        在聲音回授系列創作中，聲音不只是系統最終產生的結果，更成為了感受場域及其變化的線索，它同時暴露了空間、裝置與規則正在如何彼此作用。在後續作品中，當微生物活動、電磁訊號、網路資訊、觀眾的行為等不同因素遇上作品所建立的機制、感測、轉換及發聲方式，聲音也成為了這些因素彼此相遇時所發生的事件。聆聽不再只是接收作品所輸出的聲音，而是透過聲音察覺不同現象在系統中的相互作用。由於這些系統的即時運作，每一次聆聽都是對其自主運作過程的一次局部截取。

        **Autonomous system**        
        「自主」在這邊也是需要進一步釐清的概念。在這個系列中的「自主」目前主要指向運作上的自主（operational autonomy），以及小部分的自我調節（self-regulation），系統的基本規則仍由我建立，只是啟動之後其運作與聲音結果不仰賴人為操作。而現有作品中的不確定因素多半也只作為系統的輸入，影響其後續的聲音與運作狀態；這也留下了一個尚未在目前作品中充分展開的問題：如果系統產生的結果能進一步反過來改變自身的運作方式，不確定性與回授之間是否可能形成更複雜的自我調節關係，甚至進一步觸及系統修改自身規則（self-modification）的可能？當作品的規則在持續運作中被自身產生的結果反覆修改，經過長時間的迭代之後，如同忒修斯之船，它與藝術家最初建立的系統之間還保有什麼關係？此時「自主」或許就不再只是系統能否在沒有人為操作的情況下持續運作，而開始涉及作者最初建立的控制能在多大程度上決定作品後續的發展。而當這種關係不只發生於單一系統內部，開始存在於多個具有不同運作規則的系統之間時，系統本身的邊界也隨之成為問題。

        **System Boundaries**
        在這個系列中，有幾件作品由多個具有同樣運作邏輯的單元所構成，這些單元並不總是封閉，它們可能透過共享的空間、訊號或環境相互影響。在 *Feeding* 中，麥克風與喇叭距離的控制規則存在於每一個單元內部，然而當八座裝置同時存在於同一空間時，每個單元所發出的聲音也可能被其他單元接收，使原本各自運作的回授系統透過共享的聲學環境彼此干擾；在 *Cross* 中，這種關係則被直接建立於系統的結構之中。四個單元各自都可以被理解為一組收音與擴音裝置，但當一個單元的輸出成為下一個單元的輸入，各單元便會在循環中持續影響彼此；在 *Soul* 與 *Soul-vinyl* 中，三個主要單元各自控制不同的樂器，彼此之間沒有直接交換訊號，卻因為共同受到展場中的電磁環境影響而產生關聯； *Microbial Ensemble* 中也可以觀察到由於感測系統對周遭變化保持開放，一座裝置被觸發後所產生的震動或其他環境擾動，也可能影響其他裝置對訊號的判定。這些作品中的單元以不同程度的開放性存在於共享的環境之中；它們之間的關係有些由系統規則建立，有些透過空間與環境間接形成。

        現階段的作品雖然擴展了單元的數量，但這些單元本質上仍在同一套規則中運作。然而，如果一個系統產生的結果能成為另一個系統的輸入，或透過共享的環境、訊號與物質改變其他系統的運作，原本存在於單一系統內部的關係是否可能向外延伸，在不同系統之間形成更複雜的交互作用？這樣的狀態下，每個系統仍然保有自身的運作規則，卻同時成為其他系統所處環境的一部分；整體狀態不再由任何單一系統決定，而是在多個系統彼此局部作用的過程中形成。當系統與環境不再能被清楚分開時，系統的邊界或許也不再只是物理上的範圍，而開始涉及我們如何辨認、描述與劃分不同主體與他們之間的關係。

        **Magic**
        魔法通常由材料、咒語、情感與效果所構成，如果將它們對應到我的作品，材料是我所使用的媒介與物質，咒語是使它們彼此作用的規則與系統，情感是促使我建立這些關係的動機，聲音則是它們相遇之後產生的效果。前面的討論試圖釐清我是如何透過系統配置控制、引入不確定性，並使不同的物質與現象產生關係。而「魔法」對我而言或許正是創作的原始動機：將一個想像放進現實物質與技術的關係之中，建立一套規則，等待它產生某種連我自己也無法完全預知的效果，再觀察這些效果如何回過頭來回應最初的情感與想像。
